Product approach · Outcomes

How I decide which outcomes matter

I start with what users tell me, check it against what the data shows, and only then decide what to build and how we'll know it worked.

1. Outcome determinations 2. Feature decisions 3. Working as a quad 4. After release
  1. 1

    Listen, then look at the data

    Not every decision is user-led. What users ask for tells me where the pain is. The data tells me how big it is and who it affects.

    Always running

    Users tell me where it hurts. Data tells me how much.

    1. My SME knowledge

      20+ years in dentistry, including seven as the company's dental subject matter expert, told me where claims stall and why.

    2. Customer suggestions

      Feature requests and support tickets from practices showed which problems came up again and again.

    3. User interviews

      Conversations with billing teams showed how they actually work their claims day to day, including the workarounds.

    4. Salesforce

      Account and revenue data show which customers a problem affects and what it's worth to the business.

    5. Product usage metrics

      How practices actually use the product: which features get used, and where people get stuck or give up.

    Voice of the user Data
  2. 2

    Capture every idea, then organize

    Every request, ticket and observation goes on one board so nothing gets lost. Then I group them into focus areas we can build around.

  3. 3

    Select the outcome

    Before choosing features, I decide what the most important needle to move is. Then I set a goal. For the claims worklist, the outcomes were faster daily work and acting on claims that used to be lost.

  4. 4

    Define the metrics

    I decide how we'll measure success before we build, so the results aren't a matter of opinion. For claims, that meant tracking days in batch and time from invoice to submission.

  5. 5

    Weigh business needs

    The best ideas also move the business forward. I check each one against questions like these:

    • Will it increase monthly recurring revenue (MRR) per account?
    • Will it lower attrition caused by known problems?
    • Will it build on areas customers already celebrate?

Product approach · Features

How I make feature decisions

Once the outcome is set, I turn it into something the team can build: a clear PRD, a validated prototype, and a close partnership with engineering through release.

1. Outcome determinations 2. Feature decisions 3. Working as a quad 4. After release
  1. 1

    Build the PRD

    A PRD is never a straight path. It takes many iterations, ideas and revisions as we learn something new along the way.

    The final PRD lists the expected results, not how to get there. That leaves UX and Engineering room to find the best solution.

  2. 2

    Pictures over pages

    I'm a tactile learner and a visual decision maker. I like to build my own prototypes to help define requirements, because knowing how something should work helps me fill in the gaps.

    From small changes to new pages, I like to see it before I approve or finalize a decision. So I bring my own prototypes to product meetings.

    Using Claude to prototype makes iterations fast. Decisions no longer have to wait until someone can draw them out in Figma. We can prompt Claude, see the effect a change will have, and move quickly.

  3. 3

    Partner with engineering

    Engineering is a partner from the first idea, not a hand-off at the end.

    Before we start

    • Proof of concept. Developers build a quick, throwaway version to prove the approach works before we commit to it.
    • Backend validation. They confirm the data, APIs and database can support what the PRD asks for, and flag integration or performance limits early.
    • Project sizing. They break the work into pieces and estimate the effort, so we can plan releases and set realistic timelines.

    During the build

    • Progress reviews twice a week, moving to daily as release gets closer.
    • Developer suggestions and new learnings about what the data can do, or where we can make improvements.
    • Deprioritizing features or ideas when new code would affect other areas of the product.

Product approach · Working as a quad

How I work as a product quad

The industry calls it a product trio: product, design and engineering making decisions together instead of handing work down a line. I've added QA, so quality is designed in from the start instead of tested in at the end.

1. Outcome determinations 2. Feature decisions 3. Working as a quad 4. After release
  1. 1

    Four perspectives, one decision

    Every product decision carries risk: will customers want it, can they use it, can we build it, and does it work for the business? Adding QA covers one more: will it hold up? Each of us owns a lens, and we look at every decision through all four.

    ProductValue and viabilityIs this the right problem, and is it worth solving?
    UXUsabilityCan people actually use this?
    EngineeringFeasibilityCan we build it well, and what are the tradeoffs?
    QAQuality and riskHow could this break, and how will we know it works?
  2. 2

    Discovery, as development has time

    How it works today

    Engineering's focus time belongs to building, and our developers are still working toward a bigger role in discovery. Until then, I bring discovery to them, with short summaries and prototypes of what I'm learning.

    The goal

    Bring developers into more of discovery over time, starting when they have the bandwidth or when a question needs their expertise. Even one conversation with a real user can change how a feature gets built.

  3. 3

    Define "done" together

    Before development starts, product, engineering and QA walk through each story together.

    • We write acceptance criteria and edge cases together, often as "Given / When / Then"
    • QA brings testability and risk into the conversation early, while changes are still cheap
    • Everyone leaves with the same definition of done
  4. 4

    Build and check in together

    Progress reviews start twice a week and move to daily as release gets closer. When new risks surface, the quad decides together what to adjust, cut or deprioritize, so no one is surprised by a scope change.

    I extended our daily stand-ups by 15 minutes to build in demos and project updates. That small investment keeps the team aligned and makes iteration fast.

    See how I partner with engineering →

  5. 5

    Release together

    Quality gates are agreed up front, not negotiated at the end. We demo and review as a quad, and look back together on what to keep and what to change. Working this way helps us ship releases with consistently low defect rates.

  6. 6

    What I like

    When a developer pushes back

    Not a dev manager or a dev lead, but the developer doing the work. They see things I can't, and when they push back, it's to make sure I understand the tradeoffs and risks. It also tells me we've built a relationship on trust, where they feel safe telling me what they really think.

    When UX leads user interviews

    They ask the questions that help them understand, without my filter or lens shaping what they hear.

    When QA thinks it through in the code

    They come in with a better understanding of where things could go wrong. Their perspective helps build stronger requirements.

  7. 7

    What I avoid

    • Waterfall handoffs: product writes, design draws, engineering builds, QA finds the problems
    • Product as an order-taker, or design as decoration
    • QA seeing a feature for the first time when it is ready to test

Product approach · After release

How I work after release

Shipping isn't the finish line. After release, I work with Customer Service, Sales and Marketing so customers can use what we built, and so what they learn comes back to the product team.

1. Outcome determinations 2. Feature decisions 3. Working as a quad 4. After release
  1. 1

    Customer Service

    I train the support team before release so they can answer questions on day one: what changed, why, and how it works. Training is often ongoing, not a one-time demo.

    I'm also an active participant in solving support tickets. Partnering closely with Customer Service reps helps me know when a problem is really a problem, keeps me in the loop early, and lets me jump on solutions quickly. It also gives me great sources for client interviews.

  2. 2

    Sales

    I help Sales understand the problem a release solves and who it's for, so they can talk about the value to practices, not just the features.

    I also bridge the gap between what Sales hears and what the product does. Practices rarely describe a problem the way we do. One office asks about "claims falling through the cracks," another about "money we never got paid for." I help Sales recognize the different ways clients ask the same question, so they can connect the dots to the right solution.

  3. 3

    Marketing

    I partner with Marketing on adoption and on how a feature is represented and explained. True to pictures over pages, I sometimes bring mock logos or draft marketing articles to get the conversation started.

    My goal is to make sure we never misrepresent what a feature does, while still highlighting the benefits it brings to the practice. I also share the customer stories and feedback that show the impact.

  4. 4

    Measure and close the loop

    I track adoption against the metrics we set before building. What customers, support, sales and the data tell us feeds right back into the next round of outcome decisions.

AI · In the product

AI agents in the product

I see AI agents moving products from simply presenting data to proactively finding insights and supporting the next action, so practices spend less effort interpreting complex information.

How I use AI in my work AI agents in the product
  1. 1

    From dashboards to insights

    Traditional analytics and dashboards leave the interpreting to the user. AI can layer on top of the data a practice already has to surface insights, spot patterns, and point out what matters.

  2. 2

    From insights to next actions

    Agents can go a step further: notice a problem, recommend what to do about it, and get the work ready for a person to approve. In RCM, that means taking repetitive work, like eligibility checks and denial follow-up, off the team's plate.

  3. 3

    Keep people in control

    In healthcare, people should be taking care of people: informing, communicating and keeping it personal. Let AI do the legwork, not make the final call. I design agents to gather, compare and prepare the work, then hand the decision to a person to review and approve. That keeps trust high while the work gets faster.

  4. 4

    Making AI pay for itself

    Most software costs almost nothing extra each time someone uses it. AI is different: every action costs money each time it runs, so a popular feature can also become an expensive one. That's why I treat AI like any other investment: it has to earn more than it costs.

    Cost sets the floor, value sets the price

    I aim for a 75% gross margin on any hard cost. If an action costs us $0.10, it should bring in at least $0.40. Value to the customer decides whether we can charge more.

    Put AI where it adds the most value

    AI agent costs are still unpredictable, so I use them where they make the biggest difference, with usage limits and cost tracking per account. If a simple rule can do the job reliably, it doesn't need AI.

    One-time investment vs. ongoing cost

    Training or tuning a model is an up-front investment. Running it is an ongoing cost. Reusing knowledge the model already has, instead of starting from scratch every time, keeps each action inexpensive.

    Compare the total cost, not just the token bill

    Hard-coding a report might look cheaper, until a developer has to pull the data every month. Automating my metrics with Claude cut the time I spend on tracking by 90%, so the PM and developers can read the data, decide and move on. I weigh embedded agents the same way: the time and overhead they save can easily outweigh the tokens they use, as long as patient data stays protected and the output is checked.

  5. 5

    Where I'd take it next

    I see agents moving from working alongside the team to working ahead of it:

    Anticipate, don't just report

    Learn the patterns in a practice's own data, so problems surface on their own, before anyone has to go looking for them.

    Shorten the path to getting paid

    Help money move from the payer to the practice's books faster, with less manual work in between.

Case study · Claims (RCM phases 5–6)

From a claims report to a billing team's workspace

The starting point was a read-only list of claims. I redesigned it into a worklist that shows billers what needs attention, how much money is at stake, and lets them act without leaving the page.

Claims worklist redesign Eligibility+ Lessons and tradeoffs

What users are saying

User-led, unmonitored Facebook thread. Real users, real feedback.

What wasn't working

  1. Every claim looked the same. Status was plain text in one of ten columns, so problems didn't stand out.
  2. No dollar amounts. A $2,883 claim and a $72 claim got the same attention.
  3. Two columns, CHK/EFT number and date, were empty on every open claim.
  4. Changing the view meant editing a sentence of links.
  5. No owner or due date, so follow-up lived outside the system.
  6. No way to act on a claim from the list. Billers had to leave the worklist and find the patient's billing ledger to work the claim.
  7. Every return to the list reset all the filters, so billers rebuilt their view each time they came back.

My approach: a migration worth making

The direction

Leadership set the goal: build a new platform and move the claims list to it.

The expectation

Lift and shift the existing list. No changes, just move it.

The risk

A simple lift and shift would have cost customers without giving them anything back. They would have had to:

  • Learn new navigation for no gain
  • Learn a new interface for no gain
  • Watch their monthly subscription fund a move that didn't improve the product

My plan

Deliver full feature parity, then add enough new capability to make the change worth it: features that make billers excited to retrain their muscle memory, speed up daily work, and let them act on claims that used to be lost.

Every claim action, mapped before anything was designed

Following my product approach, I gathered SME knowledge, customer suggestions and user interviews on one board and grouped them by focus area. The notes about the claims screen became the blueprint for the worklist:

  • Easier way to identify why a claim is invalid→Invalid status pill and a Submission Issues filter
  • Maybe shortcut icons at the top of the claims screen?→Quick Filters with counts and dollar totals
  • One click to batch all claims for the day→Send All In Batch button
  • What do I want to action from the claims list?→Claim summary side panel and row actions
  • Navigate to attachments→Attachment icons on each row
  • More user friendly view of ERA and responses→ERA status icon column
See how I make feature decisions →

Decisions I made

Lead with money and workload
Workflow Status and Quick Filters show a count and a dollar total for each queue, such as Submission Issues: 150 claims, $7,733.12. The team can work the most valuable problems first.
Make status scannable
Status moved to the first column as a colored pill. Invalid claims are red and stand out at a glance.
Replace two empty columns with one ERA icon
One compact icon shows whether the remittance has arrived, been posted or needs attention. TradeoffThe EFT number is no longer visible in the list. It moved to the claim summary panel, where it's needed for posting.
Put ownership on the row
Each claim can carry an assignee and a due date ("Task Due · 7d"), so nothing waits on a sticky note.
Open claims in a side panel
The claim summary opens beside the list, so billers review details without losing their place.
Measure the flow
KPIs track average days in batch and time from invoice to submission, with trend arrows that show which way each is moving.

Case study · Eligibility (RCM phase 3)

Eligibility+

Eligibility+ brings AI-powered insurance verification into Curve, through a partnership with DentalXChange's EligibilityAI. It checks patient benefits automatically, pulling real-time, code-level coverage straight from payer portals. The direction came from the executive team. I led it end to end, from 0 to 1, and made it happen.

Claims worklist redesign Eligibility+ Lessons and tradeoffs

What users are saying

From customer feedback and testimonials.

Reported results

As published by Curve Dental for practices using Eligibility+.

15–30 min
saved per patient
40%
less admin workload
20%
boost in treatment acceptance
150%
increase in same-day acceptance

What wasn't working

  1. Staff spent hours on hold with insurance companies or jumping between payer portals.
  2. Benefits were typed in by hand, so details were easy to miss or mistype.
  3. Most checks returned only basic information, such as "active" or "inactive" coverage, not what each procedure would pay.
  4. Patients waited longer for answers and often delayed treatment.

My approach

The direction

Leadership set the goal: bring DentalXChange's EligibilityAI product to our customers.

The expectation

Send customers to the DentalXChange dashboard, or connect it to Curve, so they could see EligibilityAI's responses there.

The risk

Sending customers to a third-party dashboard offered no real benefit:

  • No advantage over using a competitor's product, like AirPay
  • Nothing connecting it to the rest of Curve
  • A look and feel that would still be disconnected

My plan

White-label EligibilityAI so it feels like a Curve product, not a DentalXChange product:

  • The UI matches the rest of Curve
  • The workflow feels familiar and intuitive
  • SmartCompare shows what the practice already knows next to what it should know, to fill in the gaps
  • SmartSync updates benefit details in one click, removing even more manual work

From problem to feature

Each problem practices faced became a feature:

  • Automation: every patient was checked manually, one at a time→Checks run automatically at three set times, so verifications are done and ready to review when staff get to work
  • Bad information already in the system→SmartCompare shows the benefits on file next to what the insurance portal reports, so staff see right away what needs updating
  • Benefits updated by hand in the practice management system→SmartSync updates benefits in one click, so accurate benefits are ready to act on
  • Updating treatment plans meant finding each plan and redoing the calculations→Treatment plans update in one click from the benefit results

Decisions I made

White-label it as a Curve product
Instead of sending customers to a partner's dashboard, EligibilityAI lives inside Curve with the same look, feel and workflow, so it reads as part of the product they already use.
Verify automatically, then filter out the noise
Eligibility+ checks insurance on its own, so staff stop waiting on hold and switching between payer portals. Quick filters, such as carrier credential errors, unsupported carriers and requests not yet sent, let staff ignore everything that's fine and pay attention only to what needs it.
Compare and contrast with SmartCompare
Instead of a simple "active" or "inactive," SmartCompare puts the benefits already in Curve next to what the payer reports, code by code, so staff can see at a glance what's wrong or missing.

Lessons and tradeoffs

Not every release lands the way I planned. These are the mistakes and compromises that changed how I work, and what I do differently because of them.

Claims worklist redesign Eligibility+ Lessons and tradeoffs

Mistakes I learned from

  1. 1

    Small doesn't mean safe

    The change
    Payment reference numbers appeared on only some payment types. We expanded them to every payment type.
    Why now
    More than 100 suggestion tickets over 10 years had asked for it, and it laid the groundwork for an RCM payment reconciliation dashboard.
    What happened
    On the printed report, the new numbers filled the right margin, where many offices wrote their notes. Upset customers took to social media, and the feedback was harsh, far out of proportion to the size of the change.
    What I learned
    Marketing and change management matter, even for the small things. A long-requested improvement can still disrupt a routine people rely on, so even small changes need a heads-up and a clear reason why.
  2. 2

    Third-party vendors can be unreliable narrators

    What happened
    In vendor conversations, we used the same words and assumed they meant the same things. A vendor would describe how something worked, I'd agree, and only later find out their definition was different from ours.
    What I learned
    Verify, verify, verify. Shared words don't guarantee shared meaning. I follow through on what a partner tells me and ask for the data that proves it before it becomes a requirement.
  3. 3

    Trust, but verify

    The request
    A DSO asked for a specific feature. I met with their account manager and one of our VPs, who explained the problem and the requirements.
    What happened
    In beta, the DSO told us it missed the mark. Our solution solved the problem for 99% of users, but not for the DSO we built it for. We had to add a whole new set of functionality to the feature.
    What I learned
    Go to the source. I should have met with the DSO directly to understand how they used this feature in their legacy software and what they expected. We could have built the full feature the first time, without the delay or the missed requirements.

Tradeoffs I made

  1. 4

    Red followed the rules. Blue followed the user.

    The starting point
    In Eligibility+, the snapshot of the day's appointments showed responses that needed attention, like carrier credential issues and basic-only responses, with red icons. UX wanted red: it followed our design guidelines and matched how we used color elsewhere in the product.
    What users told us
    Users saw red as failure. "Everything is failing." "I get so many errors." "It's not working, I need to cancel." Nothing was broken, but a wall of red made a working feature feel like a failing one.
    The tradeoff
    I pushed back. Changing to blue meant breaking from our own color rules in one place, in exchange for users trusting what they saw. It took several weeks of working with UX to get there.
    What happened
    After the change, the feedback turned around: "I like that I understand where I need to fix things." "It's easy to find the responses that need extra attention." Same feature, same results, a different icon color.
    What I learned
    Design rules exist to serve users, and sometimes the user's reaction should win. Color carries emotion, and in a product people rely on to get paid, the difference between "error" and "needs attention" changes how much they trust it.
  2. 5

    Two weeks late, months ahead

    The starting point
    We started the edit claims feature with well-defined requirements. Midway through development, we learned it also needed to support the new billing screen, and our initial back end, components and UI didn't all work there.
    The tradeoff
    Before beta, the developer and I went through the claim form one box at a time to decide whether each one supported both platforms or only RCM. We descoped anything that didn't support both, so the new billing screen could pick up the same UI. We delayed the launch by two weeks to adjust for the needs of both teams. After beta, we iterated quickly, one box at a time, until the back end and components worked with both modules.
    For example
    Add attachment was in the plan, but it only worked in RCM. We pulled it before beta and added it back once it worked in both modules.
    What happened
    The team building the new billing screen didn't have to repeat the research, requirements, development and customer learning. Overall, it saved both teams months of work and got the feature in front of users quickly.
    What I learned
    Sometimes a short delay is the faster path. Releasing what works everywhere and iterating from there beats building the same thing twice.
